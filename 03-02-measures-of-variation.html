    §3.2 Measures of Variation

    MATH 11

    One Number for the Spread

    Definition 3.2.4

    A measure of variation is a number designed to show how much the data values in a data set vary.

    The mean could not tell the two classes apart. We will study three measures of variation:

    • range
    • variance
    • standard deviation

    §3.2 Range

    MATH 11

    Biggest Minus Smallest

    Definition 3.2.5

    The range of a set of quantitative data values is the difference between the maximum data value and the minimum data value:

    Range=(max data value)−(min data value).

    Class 1: 10−4=6

    Class 2: 8−6=2

    Class 1 has the bigger range, and its dotplot looked more spread out.

    Two values in, one number out. Every other score is ignored.

    §3.2 Range

    MATH 11

    What the Range Buys You

    Pros

    • Very easy to calculate.
    • Provides a quick sense of spread.
    • Easy to understand and interpret.

    Cons

    • Not resistant. Heavily affected by outliers.
    • Only uses two data values (min and max).
    • Does not reflect variation among all data values.

    One wild score, and the range doubles. The other 14 scores never get a vote.

    §3.2 Standard Deviation

    MATH 11

    How Far From the Mean, Typically

    Definition 3.2.6

    The standard deviation of a set of quantitative data values is a measure of how much the data values deviate from the mean.

    Sample:

    s=∑(x−x¯)2n−1

    Population:

    σ=∑(x−μ)2N

    σ is read “sigma.” Same steps in both. The one difference: a sample divides by n−1, a population by N.

    §3.2 Standard Deviation

    MATH 11

    Standard Deviation by Hand

    Procedure 3.2.7 Computing the Standard Deviation by Hand

    Decide whether the data is a sample (use x¯ and divide by n−1) or a population (use μ and divide by N). The mechanical steps are identical otherwise.

    1. Compute the mean (x¯ for a sample, μ for a population).
    2. For each data value x, compute the deviation x−x¯ (or x−μ).
    3. Square each deviation to get (x−x¯)2.
    4. Sum the squared deviations: ∑(x−x¯)2.

    §3.2 Standard Deviation

    MATH 11

    Standard Deviation by Hand, Continued

    Procedure 3.2.7, continued
    1. Divide by n−1 (sample) or N (population) to get the variance.
    2. Take the square root to get the standard deviation: s=∑(x−x¯)2n−1,σ=∑(x−μ)2N.

    A table with columns x, x−x¯, (x−x¯)2 keeps the bookkeeping clean for steps 2–4.

    §3.2 Example 1

    MATH 11

    Working the Table

    Five students from a class of 40 reported their hours studied last week: 3, 5, 6, 8, 8. Find the standard deviation.

    Five of 40, so this is a sample. Mean: x¯=3+5+6+8+85=305=6.

    Data values, deviations from the mean, and squared deviations.
    Data xDeviation x−x¯Squared deviation (x−x¯)2
    33−6=−3(−3)2=9
    55−6=−1(−1)2=1
    66−6=002=0
    88−6=222=4
    88−6=222=4
    ∑(x−x¯)2=18

    §3.2 Example 1, continued

    MATH 11

    Finishing the Formula

    Sample of n=5, and the squared deviations total 18.

    s=∑(x−x¯)2n−1=185−1=4.5≈2.1 hours

    Why divide by 4 when there are 5 values?

    We had to estimate the mean from the same five numbers first. That uses up one of them, so a sample divides by n−1. Excel’s =STDEV.S does the same.

    §3.2 Standard Deviation

    MATH 11

    What the Standard Deviation Buys You

    Pros

    • Uses every data value in its calculation.
    • Same units as the original data.
    • Most commonly used measure of variation.
    • Larger values indicate greater variation; zero only when all values are the same.

    Cons

    • Not resistant. Affected by outliers.
    • More complex to calculate by hand.
    • Sample standard deviation is a (slightly) biased estimator of population standard deviation.
    • Value is never negative (cannot indicate direction).

    §3.2 Variance

    MATH 11

    The Square of the Standard Deviation

    Definition 3.2.10

    The variance of a set of quantitative data values is a measure of variation equal to the square of the standard deviation.

    Sample:

    s2=∑(x−x¯)2n−1

    Population:

    σ2=∑(x−μ)2N

    Example 1 again: s2=184=4.5 square hours. Nobody studies in square hours. That is why we usually report s.

    §3.2 Think About It

    MATH 11

    Where Do Most Scores Sit?

    An exam’s scores are bell-shaped. The mean is 70 and the standard deviation is 6.

    Between which two scores do you expect most of the class?

    “Most” needs a number. For bell-shaped data there is a rule that gives one.

    §3.2 Empirical Rule

    MATH 11

    The 68, 95, 99.7 Rule

    Theorem 3.2.12 The Empirical Rule

    If the distribution of a data set is approximately normal (bell-shaped), then

    • about 68% of all values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean,
    • about 95% of all values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean, and
    • about 99.7% of all values fall within 3 standard deviations of the mean.

    Mean 70, standard deviation 6: about 95% between 58 and 82. And the first word of the theorem is if.

    §3.2 Chebyshev’s Theorem

    MATH 11

    When the Data Is Not Bell-Shaped

    Not bell-shaped? Then the Empirical Rule does not apply. Chebyshev’s Theorem still does.

    Theorem 3.2.14 Chebyshev’s Theorem

    The proportion of any data set lying within k standard deviations of the mean is always at least 1−1k2, where k is any positive number greater than 1.

    Any shape at all. The price: it promises only at least. The true percentage is usually higher.

    §3.2 Example 2

    MATH 11

    Chebyshev Versus the Empirical Rule

    A data set has mean 70 and standard deviation 6.

    1. (a) According to Chebyshev’s Theorem, at least what percentage of the values must lie between 58 and 82?58=70−2(6) and 82=70+2(6), so k=2. 1−122=34=75%. At least 75% of the values must lie between 58 and 82.
    2. (b) Suppose the data set is bell-shaped. According to the Empirical Rule, about what percentage of the values lie between 58 and 82?58 and 82 are 2 standard deviations from the mean. About 95% of the values lie between 58 and 82.

    §3.2 Example 2, continued

    MATH 11

    Chebyshev Versus the Empirical Rule

    Same data set: mean 70 and standard deviation 6.

    1. (c) According to Chebyshev’s Theorem, at least what percentage of the values must lie between 52 and 88?52=70−3(6) and 88=70+3(6), so k=3. 1−132=89≈88.9%. At least 88.9% of the values must lie between 52 and 88.
    2. (d) Suppose the data set is bell-shaped. According to the Empirical Rule, about what percentage of the values lie between 52 and 88?52 and 88 are 3 standard deviations from the mean. About 99.7% of the values lie between 52 and 88.

    §3.2 Coefficient of Variation

    MATH 11

    Spread as a Percent of the Mean

    Definition 3.2.18

    The coefficient of variation of a data set is the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean:

    CV=sx¯(for a sample)CV=σμ(for a population).

    The coefficient of variation is normally reported as a percentage, rounded to one decimal place.

    §3.2 Coefficient of Variation

    MATH 11

    Computing the Coefficient of Variation

    Procedure 3.2.19 Computing the Coefficient of Variation

    For each data set:

    1. Compute the mean (x¯ for a sample, μ for a population).
    2. Compute the standard deviation (s for a sample, σ for a population).
    3. Compute the ratio sx¯ (or σμ).
    4. Multiply by 100% and round to one decimal place.

    Then compare the CVs across data sets. The larger CV indicates greater relative variation.

    MATH 11

    Daily sales: x¯=$900, s=$90. Customers per day: x¯=200, s=30.

    1. (a) Find the coefficient of variation for daily sales.CV=$90$900⋅100%=10.0%
    2. (b) Find the coefficient of variation for customers per day.CV=30200⋅100%=15.0%

    §3.2 Choosing

    MATH 11

    Four Measures of Spread

    When to use each measure of variation and its main weakness.
    MeasureReach for it whenWatch out
    RangeYou want a quick sense of spread.Uses only two values.
    Standard deviationAlmost always. Same units as the data.Sample or population? Pick the formula.
    VarianceA later formula asks for it.Squared units.
    Coefficient of variationDifferent units or scales.Needs a mean well away from zero.

    Rule of thumb. Report the standard deviation with the mean. Reach for the CV only when the two data sets are not on the same footing.

    §3.2 Think About It

    MATH 11

    A Standard Deviation of Zero

    Twenty values, standard deviation 0. What do you know?

    Every deviation is 0, so all 20 values equal the mean. Same number, twenty times.

    Could s ever be negative?

    No. It is the square root of a sum of squares. Zero is the floor.
