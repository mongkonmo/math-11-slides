    §5.2 Population Parameters

    MATH 11

    Population Parameters

    Since a probability distribution describes all possible values of a random variable and their probabilities, it represents an entire population rather than a sample.

    Therefore, when we calculate the mean and standard deviation, these are parameters (denoted by μ and σ), not statistics.

    • Mean: μ, not x¯
    • Standard deviation: σ, not s
    • Variance: σ2, not s2

    §5.2 Mean (Expected Value)

    MATH 11

    Mean (Expected Value)

    Theorem 5.2.1 Mean of a Discrete Probability Distribution

    The mean of a discrete probability distribution is given by μ=∑[x⋅P(x)]. This value is also called the expected value of the random variable, often denoted E(X) or simply E.

    The expected value represents the long-run average: if we repeated the experiment many times and recorded the value of X each time, the average of all those values would approach μ.

    MATH 11

    The Mean for Three Flips

    A fair coin is flipped three times. Let X represent the number of heads obtained. Find the mean of this probability distribution.

    Probability distribution for the number of heads in three flips of a fair coin, with a column for x times P of x.
    xP(x)x⋅P(x)
    00.1250⋅0.125=0.000
    10.3751⋅0.375=0.375
    20.3752⋅0.375=0.750
    30.1253⋅0.125=0.375
    Totalμ=1.500

    The mean is μ=1.5. If we flip a fair coin three times, we expect to get 1.5 heads on average.

    §5.2 Variance and Standard Deviation

    MATH 11

    Variance and Standard Deviation

    Theorem 5.2.3 Variance and Standard Deviation of a Discrete Distribution

    The variance of a discrete probability distribution is σ2=∑[(x−μ)2⋅P(x)]. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance: σ=∑[(x−μ)2⋅P(x)].

    MATH 11

    The Variance for Three Flips

    Find the variance and standard deviation for the coin flip distribution from Example 1. Round to 2 decimal places.

    We already found μ=1.5. Now we add a column for (x−μ)2⋅P(x):

    The distribution for three flips, with columns for x times P of x and for the squared distance from the mean times P of x.
    xP(x)x⋅P(x)(x−μ)2⋅P(x)
    00.1250.000(0−1.5)2⋅0.125=0.28125
    10.3750.375(1−1.5)2⋅0.375=0.09375
    20.3750.750(2−1.5)2⋅0.375=0.09375
    30.1250.375(3−1.5)2⋅0.125=0.28125
    Totalμ=1.500σ2=0.75

    The variance is σ2=0.75 and the standard deviation is σ=0.75≈0.87.

    Let X represent your net gain from the bet. We can set up a table.

    Your net gain from the bet, its probability, and x times P of x for each event.
    EventxP(x)x⋅P(x)
    Curry makes it−$1000.91−$91
    Curry misses+$5000.09+$45
    TotalE(X)=−$46

    The expected value is −$46. This means that if you took this bet many times, you would lose an average of $46 per bet. This is not a good bet for you.

    §5.2 Interpretation of Expected Value

    MATH 11

    Interpretation of Expected Value

    The expected value has important interpretations.

    1. Long-run average. If the random experiment is repeated many times, the average of all outcomes will approach the expected value.
    2. Fair value. A game or bet is considered fair if the expected value is zero—neither player has an advantage in the long run.
    3. Decision criterion. When comparing options with uncertain outcomes, the option with the higher expected value is often (but not always) preferred.

    §5.2 Interpretation of Expected Value

    MATH 11

    Expected Value and a Single Trial

    Warning 5.2.7

    Expected value represents an average over many repetitions. It does not predict what will happen on any single trial. For example, if the expected value of a game is −$0.40, you will never actually lose exactly 40 cents—you either win a prize or lose $1. The expected value only tells you the average outcome over many plays.
